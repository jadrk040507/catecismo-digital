Respect for Human Life

Big Question

Is every human life worth living? Even the one that isn't "productive"?

In 1945, after being liberated from Auschwitz, a German-Jewish doctor named Viktor Frankl asked himself a question: "Does life still have any meaning after all this?" He had lost his wife, his parents, his brother. Everything he had was a question. His answer became one of the most influential books of the 20th century: Man's Search for Meaning. He wrote: "Everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms — to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances." Human life has a value that does not depend on circumstances. Not on pain, not on illness, not on failure. It has value because it is human life. Because it is made in the image of God. Because God loves it. This is the truth the Church proclaims when she defends life: from conception to natural death, every human life is sacred.

The Fifth Commandment: "You Shall Not Kill"

The Fifth Commandment is brief and direct: "You shall not kill" (Ex 20:13). But its scope is immense. God alone is the Lord of life. No one has the right to arbitrarily take human life, one's own or another's. Life is a gift we receive from God, and only to God belongs the right to determine its end (CCC 2258-2262).

Jesus brought this commandment to its fullness in the Sermon on the Mount. It is not enough not to kill: we must not hate, insult, or despise. "You have heard that it was said to those of old: 'You shall not murder'... But I say to you: if you are angry with a brother, you will be liable to judgment" (Mt 5:21-22). The root of homicide is in the heart: anger, resentment, contempt (CCC 2262-2263).

Activity

Think about the people around you. Is there someone you hold a grudge against? Someone you have "killed" with your words, your indifference, or your contempt? The Fifth Commandment not only forbids physical killing; it calls us to respect the life and dignity of every person in thought, word, and deed.

The Dignity of Human Life

The Church teaches that every human life, from the moment of conception to natural death, is sacred because it was created by God in his image and likeness. "Before I formed you in the womb I knew you, before you were born I consecrated you" (Jer 1:5). This is the foundation of the defense of life: it is not an ideological question, but a theological one. Human life is inviolable because it belongs to God (CCC 2270-2271).

Respect for life has concrete implications for many areas of modern life. The Church calls for the protection of life at every stage: the unborn, the elderly, the sick, the disabled, the poor, the marginalized. All are made in the image of God. All deserve respect and protection (CCC 2272-2275).

Connection

Read Genesis 4:1-16: the story of Cain and Abel. Cain kills his brother out of envy. When God asks him, "Where is your brother Abel?" Cain answers, "Am I my brother's keeper?" God's response is devastating: "The voice of your brother's blood is crying to me from the ground." Every human life cries out to God. And each of us is our brother's keeper.

Abortion

The Church has held, since the earliest centuries of Christianity, that direct abortion — that is, abortion sought as an end or as a means — is a grave moral evil. The human being must be respected and treated as a person from the moment of conception. Therefore, from that same moment, the rights of the person — among which is the inviolable right of every innocent human being to life — must be recognized (CCC 2270-2271).

Abortion not only kills an innocent human being; it deeply wounds the mother, the father, the family, and society. The Church does not condemn women who have had abortions; she welcomes them with mercy and invites them to the sacrament of Reconciliation. Pope Francis has insisted that "the mercy of God reaches everyone" and that priests must be instruments of that mercy (CCC 2272-2273).

Deepening

Modern science confirms what the Church has always taught: from fertilization, the fertilized egg is a unique human being, with its own genetic code, distinct from the mother and father. It is not "a piece of the mother's body." It is an independent human life. The moral question is not "When does life begin?" but "From when must we protect it?" The Church's answer is clear: from the beginning.

Euthanasia and Care for the Sick

Euthanasia — understood as an action or omission that by its nature causes death in order to eliminate suffering — is a homicide gravely contrary to the dignity of the person and to the respect due to the living God, his Creator (CCC 2276-2279). Human life is not a good we can manage at our discretion; it is a gift we receive and are custodians of, not absolute owners.

But this does not mean we must prolong life at all costs when there is no hope of cure. The Church distinguishes between ordinary means (food, hydration, basic care) which must always be provided, and extraordinary or disproportionate means (very costly, painful, or burdensome treatments) which may legitimately be withheld (CCC 2278-2279). What is never lawful is to cause death directly, even out of compassion.

Palliative care is an expression of respect for life and Christian compassion. Accompanying the sick, relieving their pain, being present in their final moments — this is what it means to be a "good Samaritan" (CCC 2276-2279).

Expansion

The parable of the Good Samaritan (Lk 10:25-37) is the model of Christian care. The Samaritan does not ask whether the injured man "deserves" help. He does not evaluate his productivity, his origin, or his religion. He simply sees a human being who is suffering and helps. This is how we must see every person: not as a problem, but as a brother or sister who needs our love.

The Death Penalty, Self-Defense, and War

The Church's teaching on the death penalty has evolved toward a position of total abolition. Pope Francis modified the Catechism in 2018 to affirm that "the death penalty is inadmissible because it attacks the inviolability and dignity of the person" (CCC 2267). Today there are sufficient means to defend society without resorting to the execution of criminals.

Self-defense, on the other hand, is a right and a duty. Those who have the responsibility to protect innocent lives — police, soldiers, parents — may use proportionate force to repel an unjust aggression. The intention is not to kill, but to defend. If the death of the aggressor results as an unintended side effect, it is not morally imputable (CCC 2263-2265).

War is a tragedy that must be avoided at all costs. The Church teaches that there are strict conditions for a war to be morally justifiable, but at the same time calls leaders to always seek the path of peace and dialogue before the path of arms (CCC 2302-2317).

Context

Jesus said: "Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God" (Mt 5:9). Peace is not simply the absence of war. It is the tranquility of order, justice, and freedom. St. John Paul II said: "Peace is the fruit of justice." There can be no peace where human rights are violated, where the poor are oppressed, where the innocent are killed.

Biblical Connection: Jesus, Lord of Life

"I came that they may have life, and have it abundantly."

John 10:10

Jesus does not merely respect life; He is Life. In the Gospels, Jesus restores life wherever He finds death: He raises Jairus's daughter (Mk 5:21-43), the son of the widow of Nain (Lk 7:11-17), his friend Lazarus (Jn 11). Each miracle is a preview of His own resurrection, the definitive victory over death.

The defense of life is not one "issue" among many. It is the foundation of the entire moral teaching of the Church. Without respect for life, there is respect for nothing else. This is why saints have always been defenders of life: from St. John Paul II, who dedicated his encyclical Evangelium Vitae to the Gospel of Life, to Mother Teresa, who gathered dying people from the streets of Calcutta because "every life is worth living with dignity."

For Reflection

  1. What does it mean to you that human life is "sacred"? How does this change the way you see the people around you?
  2. The Fifth Commandment forbids not only killing, but also hating and despising. Is there someone you need to forgive in your heart?
  3. The Church defends life from conception to natural death. What challenges do you see in today's culture for living this teaching?
  4. If a non-Christian friend asked you why the Church opposes abortion and euthanasia, what would you say?
  5. How can you be, in your environment, an advocate for life? What concrete actions could you take?

Key Idea

Key Idea: Every human life is sacred because it is created by God in His image. Defending life is the first work of mercy: to give life, protect it, care for it, accompany it from the beginning to the end.

Cultural Riches

The Scream by Edvard Munch — This expressionist painting shows an anguished figure under a red sky. Munch wrote that he was inspired by a sunset that felt like "an infinite scream of nature." It is the image of human suffering, of existential anguish. The Church does not ignore that scream. She listens to it and responds with the truth that life has meaning, even in the midst of suffering, because it is a gift from God.

Evangelium Vitae by St. John Paul II (1995) — This encyclical is one of the most important documents on the defense of life. It opens with a powerful phrase: "The Gospel of life is at the heart of Jesus' message." John Paul II defends life with theological, philosophical, scientific, and cultural arguments. Essential reading for understanding the Church's teaching on human life.

St. Francis of Assisi and Respect for Creation — St. Francis called the sun "brother" and the moon "sister." His Canticle of the Creatures celebrates life in all its forms. For Francis, all creation is a sign of God's love. Respect for human life cannot be separated from respect for all creation. "Praised be you, my Lord, through sister death," Francis sang, knowing that earthly life ends, but life in God has no end.
