Catechist's Guide — The Virtues

Catechist's Guide

For preparing and leading the class.

Class Summary

Estimated time: 60 minutes

Core topic: Virtues are habitual dispositions to do good. The cardinal virtues are acquired through practice; the theological virtues are received as gifts from God.

Objectives:

  • Understand what a virtue is and how it is acquired.
  • Identify the four cardinal virtues and their importance.
  • Distinguish the theological virtues from the cardinal virtues.
  • Know the gifts and fruits of the Holy Spirit.

Key verse: «Put on compassion, kindness, humility, meekness, and patience» (Col 3:12)

Catechism references: CCC 1803-1845

Suggested Class Structure

Section Time What the Catechist Does
1. Big Question 10 min Pianist story. Ask: «What have you learned through practice?»
2. Cardinal Virtues 15 min Explain each with examples. Ask students for examples of prudent, just, strong, temperate people.
3. Theological Virtues 12 min Faith, hope, charity. Emphasis on them being gifts. Relationship with the Trinity.
4. Gifts & Fruits 8 min Quick explanation. Ask: «Which fruit of the Spirit do you need most?»
5. Biblical Connection 5 min Col 3:12-14 and 2 Pet 1:5-7. Read aloud.
6. Reflection & Close 10 min Choose ONE virtue to practice this week.

Notes for the Catechist

Key teaching: Virtues are not a «to-do list.» They are a way of being. Catechesis on virtues should be positive: it’s not about prohibition, but about order. Virtue makes us free to love better.

St. Thomas on prudence: For Aquinas, prudence is the «mother of the virtues» because without it, no other virtue can be exercised well. The prudent person knows when, how, and why to act. It is the virtue of discernment.

Charity as the «form of the virtues»: St. Thomas teaches that charity gives form to all other virtues. Without charity, prudence can become cunning, justice can become rigidity, fortitude can become stubbornness, temperance can become coldness. Charity orders everything to the love of God.

Age Adaptation

🌱 Seed (Ages 7-12)

  • Use the sports metaphor: virtues are like training to be a «champion of good.»
  • Teach the 4 cardinal virtues as the 4 pillars of a house.
  • Activity: draw a house with 4 pillars, write a virtue on each.

🌿 Sprout (Ages 13-17)

  • Connect to their reality: «What virtue do you need to resist peer pressure? Fortitude? Prudence?»
  • Discuss how virtues are practiced in small daily decisions.

🪴 Root (Adults new in faith)

  • May come from a context where morality was just «rules.»
  • Help them see virtues as a path to freedom, not restriction.

🌳 Tree (Formed adults)

  • Develop the relationship between theological virtues and the gifts of the Holy Spirit.
  • Discuss whether non-Christians can live the cardinal virtues. What do they lack without charity?

Difficult Questions

1. «Aren’t virtues just for saints? I can’t be perfect.»

Virtues are not instant perfection. They are a path. No one is born virtuous. What matters is not being perfect, but being on the way. A small act of virtue each day brings you closer to God.

2. «What if I don’t have faith? Can I be virtuous without God?»

Yes, you can have cardinal virtues without explicit faith. Many non-Christians live these virtues well. But without charity (a gift from God), these virtues don’t lead to union with God. They are like a car without fuel: it works, but doesn’t reach its final destination.

3. «Sometimes I do good and feel nothing. Does that mean I don’t have virtue?»

No. Virtue is not a feeling; it is a habit. You can do good without feeling anything and still grow in virtue. In fact, virtuous acts done when you don’t «feel» them are the ones that most strengthen the virtue.

Catechist’s Prayer

Lord God, source of all virtue,
You who have called us to be holy,
grant us the grace to grow in the virtues.
May we be prudent in our decisions,
just in our actions,
strong in difficulties,
and temperate in the use of Your gifts.
Increase in us faith, hope, and charity,
and make us bear fruit for eternal life.
Amen.
