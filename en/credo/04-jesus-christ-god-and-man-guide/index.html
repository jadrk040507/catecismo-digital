Catechist's Guide — Jesus Christ, God and Man

Catechist's Guide

For class preparation and delivery. For the catechist's use only.

Class Summary

Estimated duration: 60 minutes

Central theme: Jesus is true God and true man; in him is the salvation of humanity found.

Objectives:

  • Understand that Jesus is true God, consubstantial with the Father (CCC 464-465).
  • Recognize that Jesus is true man, like us in all things but sin (CCC 470-471).
  • Understand the hypostatic union: two natures (divine and human) in one Person (CCC 467-468).
  • Discover the mystery of the kenosis: God emptied himself for love of us (CCC 471-472).
  • Inspire personal confession of faith: "But what about you? Who do you say I am?" (Mk 8:29).

Key verse: "Who, being in very nature God, did not consider equality with God something to be used to his own advantage, but made himself nothing" (Phil 2:6-7)

CCC references: 422-483

Suggested Class Structure

Section Duration What the catechist does
1. Big Question 8 min Read Mk 8:27-30. Pose: "Who do people say I am?" Then: "But what about you? Who do you say I am?" Listen to spontaneous answers.
2. The name and the mission: Jesus 8 min Explain the meaning of "Jesus" (God saves) and "Christ" (Anointed). Activity: proper names and their meanings.
3. True God: the Son of the Father 12 min Read Jn 1:1,14. Explain the Council of Nicaea. The Arian heresy: why does it matter that Jesus is true God?
4. True man: the hypostatic union 12 min Explain the humanity of Jesus with Heb 4:15. The Council of Chalcedon: two natures, one Person. Why do we need Jesus to be man?
5. The kenosis: God emptied himself 10 min Read Phil 2:6-11. Explain the kenosis as the maximum expression of love. Activity: imagine the renunciations of Jesus in becoming incarnate.
6. Biblical Connection: Peter confesses 5 min Return to Mk 8. Peter succeeds and fails: he confesses but does not understand the cross. How far does our confession go?
7. For Reflection 5 min Small groups or brief plenary with 2-3 reflection questions.

Notes for the Catechist

The Christology of Nicaea

The Council of Nicaea (325) is the starting point of Christian orthodoxy. Arianism said the Son was "of a similar substance" to the Father, but not consubstantial. Nicaea responded: "of the same substance" (homoousios). This is not an abstract theological discussion: if Jesus is not true God, he cannot save us. Only God can reconcile humanity with himself.

The kenosis as pastoral key

The kenosis (Phil 2:6-7) is the mystery that most connects with the human heart. God did not come in power but in fragility; he did not dominate but served. This is revolutionary in our culture of domination and success. The kenosis is also the model of the Christian life: "Have the same mindset as Christ Jesus" (Phil 2:5). The imitation of Christ begins here.

The hypostatic union: a mystery to be lived

"Hypostatic union" sounds technical, but it is the key to our hope. In Christ, the human and the divine meet without being confused. That means that our humanity can be assumed by God, transformed, divinized. The Incarnation is not a "disguise" of God; it is the marriage between the divine and the human. And we are invited to that union.

Jesus' question today

"But what about you? Who do you say I am?" is the question that every Christian must answer with their life. It is not enough to repeat the Creed; we must do it "with the heart to be just" (Rom 10:10). The task of the catechist is not to transfer information about Christ, but to facilitate the encounter with Christ. If at the end of the class the participants do not feel challenged to a personal response, something has been missing.

Age Adaptation

🌱 Seed (ages 7-12)

Use simple analogies: Jesus is "100% God and 100% man" like a coin that has two sides but is one coin. Activity: make a "kenosis collage" with images of baby, carpenter, cross. Memorize Jn 1:14 with drawings.

🌿 Sprouts (ages 13-17)

Connect with the question of identity proper to adolescence. "Who are you?" is the question they ask themselves. "Who is Jesus?" is the question God asks them. Use the image of the "friend who gives his life" (Jn 15:13). Do they know someone who would be willing to die for them? Have they known someone who did die for them?

🪴 Root (adults new to the faith)

Many come with a vague image of Jesus as a "moral teacher." Help them take the step to Christian faith: Jesus is not only a model to follow, but God who comes to save us. The step from "admire Jesus" to "entrust your life to Jesus" is the step of the catechumenate.

🌳 Tree (formed adults)

Go deeper into the patristic texts: Athanasius "God became man so that man might become God." Irenaeus "The Word of God became what we are to make us what he is." Divinization as the destiny of the Christian. You can cite the Church Fathers and connect with the spirituality of union with Christ.

Difficult Questions

"Why did God become man? Could he not save us in another way?"

Saint Anselm formulated the classical answer: God could save us in many ways, but he chose this because it was the worthiest of his love. He does not save us by pure force, but by love; and love makes itself close, enters our condition, shares our pain. Moreover, only a human being could represent humanity; and only God could save. That is why we need a Mediator who is both (CCC 460-461).

"How can someone be 100% God and 100% man? That sums to 200%."

The coin analogy helps: it has two sides but is one coin. Jesus has two natures but is one Person. He is not "half and half," nor "one part and another part." He is completely God and completely man in every act. The divine does not mix or confuse the human; the human does not limit the divine (CCC 467-468).

"If Jesus was God, why did he pray? Why did he say he did not know the day of judgment?"

As man, Jesus had limited human consciousness (called "beatific consciousness" or "infused knowledge" in theology). As divine, he knew everything. In his humanity, he experienced growth, limited ignorance, suffering. All of this is part of his kenosis: he submitted to real human conditions in order to save us from within (CCC 472-473).

"Why is the divinity of Christ so important? Is it not enough that he was a great teacher?"

C.S. Lewis posed this dilemma: Jesus is either God, or he is mad, or he is a liar. He cannot be "just a great teacher" if he claims to be God. If he is not God, his preaching is arrogant or delirious. If he is God, his preaching is the most important truth in history. Christians do not worship a teacher; they worship God made man (CCC 426-429, 465).

Catechist's Prayer

Lord Jesus,
you who being God became man,
who emptied your glory to find me,
teach me to speak of you with the truth of the Creed
but also with the fire of love.
May those who listen not remain only in ideas,
but encounter you, living Lord,
true God and true man,
only Savior of the world.
Amen.
