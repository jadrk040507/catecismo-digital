The Liturgy: Encounter with Christ

Big Question

What makes an ordinary gathering become an unforgettable encounter?

Picture this scene. A table set. Bread broken. A conversation that starts nervously and ends in tears. Two disciples were walking back to Emmaus, hearts broken, talking about everything that had happened. Everything seemed lost. And then a stranger joins them on the road, explains the Scriptures to them, sits at the table with them, breaks the bread… and in that simple gesture, their eyes are opened. They recognize him. It is Jesus. And in an instant everything changes: «Were not our hearts burning within us while he talked with us on the road?» (Lk 24:32). That is the liturgy: not an empty ritual, not a Sunday obligation. It is the table where Jesus breaks bread for us. It is the place where our hearts start burning again.

What Is the Liturgy?

The word liturgy comes from the Greek leitourgia, meaning «public work» or «service on behalf of the people.» In the Church, the liturgy is the work of Christ and of his Body, the Church, through which the work of our redemption is continued in time (CCC 1069). It is not a human invention, not a set of arbitrary rules. The liturgy is the participation of the People of God in the work of God himself.

The Catechism explains that in the liturgy, Christ himself acts: it is he who baptizes, he who forgives, he who offers himself in the Eucharist. The priest acts «in the person of Christ» (in persona Christi), but it is Christ who truly works (CCC 1085). That is why the liturgy does not depend on our emotions, on the quality of the choir, or on the homily. It depends on Christ, who is always present and active.

Activity

Think of the last time you went to Mass. What caught your attention most? The singing, the silence, the readings, the people? Now ask yourself: did you feel that Christ was there? What would you change to notice him more?

The liturgy has two inseparable dimensions: it is the work of Christ (divine action) and the work of the Church (human action). As St. Augustine says, when we sing, when we pray, when we offer the bread and wine, it is Christ who sings, who prays, who offers himself in us (CCC 1088). The liturgy is the place where heaven touches earth.

The Paschal Mystery, Heart of the Liturgy

Everything the liturgy celebrates is the Paschal Mystery of Christ: his passion, death, resurrection, and ascension. We do not simply remember something that happened two thousand years ago; we make that mystery present, here and now. In every Mass, the same sacrifice of Christ is made present sacramentally (CCC 1085).

The Church Fathers used to say that the liturgy is «the Pasch of Christ prolonged in time.» Just as the Exodus freed Israel from slavery in Egypt, the Pasch of Christ frees us from sin and death. And that liberation is applied to us in every sacrament, especially in the Eucharist (CCC 1090).

Connection

Read Exodus 12:1-28 (the Jewish Passover and the paschal lamb). Then read 1 Corinthians 5:7: «Christ, our Passover, has been sacrificed.» What connections do you see between the lamb of Exodus and Christ in the Eucharist? How does the Old Testament illuminate what we celebrate at Mass?

The Celebrating Church: Common and Ministerial Priesthood

All the baptized participate in the liturgy. Not as spectators, but as active members of the Body of Christ. The Second Vatican Council insisted that the liturgy is «the summit toward which all the activity of the Church is directed; it is also the source from which all its power flows» (SC 10). But not everyone participates in the same way.

There is a common priesthood of all the faithful: each baptized person, by virtue of baptism, is called to offer his or her life as a spiritual sacrifice (CCC 1141-1143). And there is the ministerial priesthood of bishops and priests, who act in the name of Christ the Head to celebrate the sacraments (CCC 1142). Both are necessary, both are complementary. The ministerial priesthood is at the service of the common priesthood.

Context

At Mass, when the priest says «The Lord be with you» and you answer «And with your spirit,» you are exercising your common priesthood. You are not a passive spectator. The liturgy is a dialogue, not a monologue. How would your participation at Mass change if you remembered that you too are a protagonist?

Time and Signs: How the Church Celebrates

The Church celebrates the liturgy through signs and symbols: water, bread, wine, oil, the laying on of hands, incense, light. We are body and soul, and we need visible signs to encounter the invisible reality of God. Jesus himself used signs: mud, bread, water (CCC 1145-1152).

The Church also celebrates in time. The Liturgical Year is not just any calendar; it is the unfolding of the whole life of Christ throughout the year: Advent, Christmas, Lent, Easter, Ordinary Time. Every Sunday is a «little Easter» (CCC 1163-1173). And the Liturgy of the Hours sanctifies the entire day with the prayer of the Church (CCC 1174-1178).

Going Deeper

The liturgical year is not a meaningless cycle that repeats itself. It is an ascending spiral: each year we enter more deeply into the mystery of Christ. How could you live the next Advent or Lent not as «here we go again,» but as a new opportunity for encounter with Christ? What liturgical sign or symbol helps you most to feel the presence of God?

Biblical Connection: The First Christian Community

«They devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and to fellowship, to the breaking of bread and to prayer… All the believers were together and had everything in common… They broke bread in their homes and ate together with glad and sincere hearts, praising God.»

Acts 2:42-47

Here we find the four pillars of the Church's life: the apostles' teaching (doctrine), fellowship (koinonia), the breaking of bread (Eucharist), and prayer. The liturgy is not just the rite; it is the entire life of the Church lived in communion with Christ and with one another. You cannot separate Mass from love of neighbor. You cannot separate prayer from charity (CCC 1108-1109).

For Reflection

  1. What did the word «liturgy» mean to you before this lesson? What does it mean now?
  2. Have you ever experienced in the Mass that «burning heart» that the disciples of Emmaus spoke of? When?
  3. If the liturgy is the work of Christ and not ours, how does that change your attitude when something «doesn't please you» in the celebration?
  4. The common priesthood of the faithful makes you a protagonist of the liturgy. How can you participate more actively in Mass?
  5. What liturgical sign (water, light, incense, bread, wine) helps you most to feel God's presence? Why?

Key Takeaway

Key Takeaway: The liturgy is not what we do for God, but what God does for us in Christ, to which we respond with praise and thanksgiving.

Cultural Resources

«The Mass of Bolsena» by Raphael — In the Vatican Stanze, Raphael painted the scene of a priest who doubted the real presence of Christ in the Eucharist and, upon breaking the host, saw blood flow. It is a powerful image of what the liturgy offers us: the certainty that Christ is truly present. Not a symbol. An encounter.

«The Brothers Karamazov» by Dostoevsky — In this novel, Father Zosima, a holy monk, teaches that the liturgy is not a rite separate from life, but that Mass must transform the way we treat others. «Every time you sit at the table, remember that Christ breaks bread with you,» he says. Liturgy and life cannot be separated.

Gregorian Chant: «Ubi Caritas» — This chant, sung on Holy Thursday, expresses the essence of Christian liturgy: «Where charity and love are, God is there.» The liturgy is not a show; it is the expression of God's love that gathers us and transforms us. Gregorian chant, with its simplicity and beauty, helps us enter into the mystery.
