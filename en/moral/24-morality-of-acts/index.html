The Morality of Human Acts

Big Question

What makes an action good or bad? Is it what you do, why you do it, or what happens as a result?

A woman named Maria gave a generous donation to a charity. Everyone praised her. But Maria knew something nobody else knew: she gave the money not out of compassion, but because she wanted her picture in the newspaper. The act looked good, but the intention was self-serving. Does that make the act less good? What if, despite her selfish motive, the charity still fed a hundred children with her donation? This is the question of the morality of human acts: we are not just what we do, but also why we do it. And as the Letter of James reminds us, faith without works is dead, but works without love are empty (1 Cor 13:1-3).

The Three Sources of Morality

The Catechism teaches that every human act draws its moral character from three sources, and all three must be good for the act to be good (CCC 1749-1750):

1. The Object Chosen

The object is the «what» of the act — the specific action itself. Some actions are always wrong regardless of intention or circumstances, because their object is intrinsically evil: murder, adultery, theft, lying. «There are acts which, in and of themselves, independently of circumstances and intentions, are always gravely illicit» (CCC 1756). The object gives the act its primary moral species.

2. The Intention

The intention is the «why» of the act — the purpose or goal you have in doing it. A good intention (like helping someone) cannot make an evil act good. You cannot lie «for a good cause» (CCC 1753). However, a bad intention can corrupt an otherwise good act. Giving to the poor to be seen (Mt 6:2) is less virtuous than giving out of genuine love.

3. The Circumstances

Circumstances are the «who, where, when, how» of the act. They can increase or decrease the goodness or evil of an act, but they cannot change its fundamental moral quality. Stealing bread to feed a starving family is still theft, though the circumstances may diminish responsibility (CCC 1754-1755).

Activity

Think of a recent decision you made. Ask yourself: What did I do (object)? Why did I do it (intention)? What were the circumstances? Do all three line up with what is good?

Acting with Knowledge and Will

For an act to be truly human — and therefore morally accountable — it must proceed from knowledge and free will. Acts done in ignorance, under duress, or without consent are not fully human acts and therefore carry less or no moral responsibility (CCC 1745-1748).

However, the Church distinguishes between invincible ignorance (which you could not overcome) and vincible ignorance (which you could and should have overcome). If you are ignorant because you chose not to learn, that ignorance does not excuse you. The parable of the servants in Matthew 25:14-30 illustrates this: the servant who «did not know» was still held accountable because he should have known.

Good Acts and Evil Acts

An act is morally good when all three sources — object, intention, and circumstances — are good. If even one is evil, the act is evil. «A morally good act requires the goodness of the object, of the end, and of the circumstances together» (CCC 1755). This is a demanding standard, but also a liberating one: it means that when you act with a pure heart, seeking the good, even your imperfect efforts are pleasing to God.

St. Thomas Aquinas taught that human actions are moral actions precisely because they are free and deliberate. Animals act by instinct; humans act by choice. And because we can choose, we are responsible. This is what makes us image-bearers of God: the capacity to know the truth and freely choose the good (CCC 1730-1734).

Connection

Some actions are so gravely evil that no intention or circumstance can make them good. The Church calls these intrinsically evil acts (CCC 1756). This is why torture, genocide, abortion, and adultery can never be justified, no matter how «good» the intention. The end does not justify the means.

Indirectly Voluntary Acts

What about things we did not directly intend, but happened as a result of our actions? The Catholic moral tradition speaks of the «double effect» of actions (CCC 1754-1756). An action can have two effects: one good (intended) and one evil (unintended). For the act to be permissible, four conditions must be met:

  1. The act itself must be good or neutral.
  2. The good effect must be intended, not the evil one.
  3. The good effect must not come about through the evil effect.
  4. There must be a proportionately grave reason for permitting the evil effect.

For example, a doctor who prescribes pain relief to a dying patient (intending to ease pain) may inadvertently shorten the patient’s life (unintended). The act can be permissible if the intent is to relieve suffering and the dose is proportionate. This is not euthanasia; it is accepting an unintended side effect of doing good.

Deepening

The principle of double effect, formulated by St. Thomas Aquinas (Summa Theologiae, II-II, q.64, a.7), has become a cornerstone of Catholic moral teaching, especially in bioethics. It prevents a consequentialist ethic where «the end justifies the means» while still allowing for the complexity of real moral decisions.

Biblical Connection: Trees and Their Fruit

«Either make the tree good and its fruit good, or make the tree bad and its fruit bad; for the tree is known by its fruit. You brood of vipers! How can you speak good, when you are evil? For out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks. The good person out of his good treasure brings forth good, and the evil person out of his evil treasure brings forth evil»

Matthew 12:33-35

Jesus teaches that our actions flow from who we are. A good tree produces good fruit; an evil tree, evil fruit. The morality of our acts is not just about following rules — it is about becoming the kind of person from whom good actions naturally flow. This is why virtue matters, and why we need grace. «For I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want is what I keep on doing» (Rom 7:19). Only Christ can set us free.

For Reflection

  1. Think of a time you did something that looked good on the outside but had a mixed intention. How did that feel?
  2. Have you ever justified a wrong action by saying «my intention was good»? What does the Church teach about this?
  3. How do you form your conscience to know whether an action is good or evil?
  4. What does it mean to you that you are responsible for not only what you do, but also what you could have known?
  5. How does the principle of double effect help you think about complex moral decisions?

Key Idea

Key Idea: Every human act has three moral sources: object, intention, and circumstances. All three must be good for the act to be good. You are responsible for what you do, why you do it, and what you could have known.

Cultural Treasure

Dostoevsky’s «Crime and Punishment» — Raskolnikov murders an old woman believing he is an «extraordinary man» above ordinary morality. His intention seems noble (to use her money for good), but the object (murder) is intrinsically evil. The novel is a masterful exploration of how a bad act corrupts the soul, no matter how good the intention. Raskolnikov finds peace only when he confesses his crime and accepts the consequences, proving that the truth of our acts cannot be escaped.

Sophocles’ «Antigone» — Antigone defies the king’s decree to bury her brother, following a higher law: the unwritten law of God. The play dramatizes the conflict between human law and the natural moral law, and the duty of conscience to follow God rather than men (Acts 5:29).
