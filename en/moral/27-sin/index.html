Sin

Big Question

What happens when you say «yes» to the evil you know is evil?

There was once a man who lived in a small village. He was known for his honesty. One day, he found a bag of money on the road. The bag contained a whole year’s wages for a poor family. The man knew whose bag it was. He knew returning it was the right thing. But he thought of his own debts, his own needs. And he decided to keep it. That night he could not sleep. The bag was under his bed, but it felt like it weighed a ton. That is what sin is like. It is not just breaking a rule. It is carrying something that will not let you rest. It is knowing you could have done good and chose evil. But the story does not end there. The owner of the bag, upon learning what happened, did not report him. He went to him and said: «Brother, if you need the money, keep it. But give me back my peace.» The man wept and returned the bag. Sin exists, but mercy is always greater.

The Reality of Sin

The Catechism defines sin as «a failure against reason, truth, and right conscience; it is a transgression of God’s law» (CCC 1849). At its heart, sin is an act of unloving: choosing yourself over God and neighbor. It is saying to God: «I know what you want from me, but I prefer what I want.»

Sin wounds our relationship with God, with others, with ourselves, and with creation. «Every sin is a disobedience to God and a lack of trust in His goodness» (CCC 1850). It is not just a mistake: it is an act of freedom that chooses evil knowing it is evil.

It is important to distinguish between original sin (the condition inherited from Adam, which inclines us toward evil but is not a personal act) and actual sins (concrete acts we freely commit). Original sin is a wound we receive; actual sins are wounds we inflict (CCC 1846-1848).

Activity

Think of a time you did something you knew was wrong. How did you feel afterward? What did you need in that moment? Punishment or a hug?

Mortal Sin and Venial Sin

The Church distinguishes between mortal sin and venial sin. This distinction is not a way of ranking sins as «serious» and «minor,» but of understanding how each sin affects our relationship with God (CCC 1854-1855).

Mortal Sin

Mortal sin is a radical break with God. It is called «mortal» because it kills the life of grace in the soul. For a sin to be mortal, three conditions must be met (CCC 1857):

  1. Grave matter: the act must be seriously evil (murder, adultery, grave theft, apostasy, etc.).
  2. Full knowledge: you know it is evil and that it is grave.
  3. Deliberate consent: you freely choose it, without coercion.

If any one of these conditions is lacking, the sin is not mortal. Mortal sin separates us from communion with God. But even mortal sin can be forgiven in Confession. God never abandons anyone.

Venial Sin

Venial sin is a minor offense that does not break friendship with God but weakens it (CCC 1862-1863). It is like a small crack in a wall: it doesn’t bring the house down, but it weakens it. Examples: a white lie, a moment of impatience, a passing thought of envy. Repeated venial sins accustom us to evil and make us more vulnerable to mortal sin.

Connection

Have you ever heard someone say «it’s just a venial sin, no big deal»? St. Josemaria Escriva said: «Do not despise small faults: they are like earth, which though not heavy, little by little fills the boat and sinks it.» Repeated venial sins create a routine of evil that makes us less sensitive to grace.

The Capital Vices and Sins That Cry to Heaven

The Church’s tradition identifies seven capital vices (or deadly sins) that are the root of many other sins (CCC 1865-1866):

  • Pride: considering yourself superior to others and to God.
  • Greed: disordered desire to possess.
  • Lust: disordered desire for sexual pleasure.
  • Envy: sadness at the good of another.
  • Gluttony: disordered desire for food and drink.
  • Wrath: disordered desire for vengeance.
  • Sloth: spiritual laziness, negligence in serving God.

Scripture also mentions four sins whose gravity is such that they «cry to heaven» (CCC 1867):

  • Willful murder (Gen 4:10)
  • Sodomy (Gen 18:20)
  • Oppression of the poor (Ex 2:23)
  • Defrauding workers of their wages (Jas 5:4)

Deepening

The Desert Fathers (Evagrius Ponticus, Cassian) were the first to systematize the «eight evil thoughts» that later became the seven deadly sins. Gregory the Great reduced them to seven. Thomas Aquinas organized them according to their «end»: each capital vice is the disordered pursuit of a good (pleasure, possession, power) that only finds its right order in God.

Mercy Is Always Greater

All of the above might sound frightening, but there is a greater truth: «Where sin abounded, grace abounded all the more» (Rom 5:20). God never tires of forgiving. We are the ones who tire of asking for forgiveness.

Sin does not have the last word. The last word belongs to God’s mercy. The Catechism states clearly: «Mortal sin, though it deprives us of communion with God, does not deprive us of the possibility of repenting and recovering grace» (CCC 1861). While there is life, there is hope.

St. John Paul II said: «Do not be afraid. Open wide the doors to Christ.» Even after the gravest sin, Christ is there, with open arms, waiting for us like the Father of the prodigal son.

Deepening

St. Faustina Kowalska received the message of Divine Mercy: «The soul that trusts in My mercy is the happiest, because I myself take care of it.» The Church has established Divine Mercy Sunday (the Second Sunday of Easter) as a reminder that mercy is God’s greatest attribute.

Biblical Connection: The Prodigal Son

«There was a man who had two sons. The younger said to his father, ‘Father, give me the share of property that is coming to me.’ And he divided his property between them. Not many days later, the younger son gathered all he had and took a journey into a far country, and there he squandered his property in reckless living...»

Luke 15:11-13

The parable of the prodigal son (Lk 15:11-32) is the best summary of the Catholic teaching on sin and mercy. The prodigal son represents each of us when we choose sin. The father represents God, who does not hold us back, who respects our freedom, but waits for us with open arms every time we decide to come home.

St. John reminds us: «If we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us. If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness» (1 Jn 1:8-9). This is our hope.

For Reflection

  1. What sins do you find hardest to admit? The grave ones or the «little» ones?
  2. Have you experienced God’s mercy in the sacrament of Confession? How did it feel?
  3. Is there a sin you keep repeating that you feel you cannot leave? What could help you?
  4. How do you distinguish between the sin you commit and the person you are?
  5. «Where sin abounded, grace abounded all the more.» What does this mean for your life?

Key Idea

Key Idea: Sin is real and wounds our relationship with God, but God’s mercy is infinitely greater. It is never too late to come home.

Cultural Treasure

Dostoevsky’s «Crime and Punishment» — Rodion Raskolnikov murders an old woman convinced that «extraordinary men» are above ordinary morality. But sin does not liberate him: it torments him. He only finds peace when he confesses his crime and accepts the punishment. Dostoevsky understood that sin is not just a legal transgression, but an illness of the soul that can only be cured by repentance and mercy.

Dostoevsky’s «The Grand Inquisitor» — In this famous parable within «The Brothers Karamazov,» Christ returns to earth and is imprisoned by the Inquisitor, who tells him: «Why have you come to disturb us?» The Inquisitor prefers a world without freedom (and without sin) to a world where men can sin but also love freely. Dostoevsky shows that sin is the price of freedom, but that God respects that freedom to the extreme.
