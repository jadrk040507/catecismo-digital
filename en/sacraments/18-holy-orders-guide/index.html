Catechist Guide — Holy Orders

1. Session Summary

Duration: 60 minutes

Central Theme: Holy Orders as the sacrament that configures men to act in the person of Christ the Head.

Objectives:

  • Understand the difference between the common priesthood and the ministerial priesthood.
  • Explain the three degrees of Orders and their distinct functions.
  • Communicate the value of the priestly ministry as service, not power.

2. Suggested Structure

Section Duration Suggested Activity
Opening 10 min Present the Big Question: Why didn't Jesus just leave a book? Discuss why Christ chose persons over documents to continue his mission.
Doctrine 20 min Explain the two priesthoods (common and ministerial) and the three degrees of Orders: bishop, priest, and deacon. Use the image of the body where different members have different functions.
Deepening 15 min Reflect on the effects of the sacrament: the indelible character and the grace of the Holy Spirit. Address the question of priestly celibacy if the group is mature enough. Use the "depth boxes" from the lesson for different age levels.
Reflection 10 min Give space for the workbook questions. Encourage honest reflection on their experience of priests they have known.
Closing 5 min Catechist prays aloud for the bishops, priests, and deacons of the diocese. Invite participants to commit to praying for priestly vocations during the coming week.

3. Notes for the Catechist

Sensitivity Regarding the Male Priesthood: This topic can raise questions about why only men are ordained. Treat the question with respect and clarity. Explain that the Church's practice does not stem from any undervaluing of women—Mary, the Mother of God, exceeds all the apostles and popes in holiness and dignity. Rather, the Church is bound by Christ's own choice: he selected twelve men as Apostles, and the priest sacramentally represents Christ as the Bridegroom of the Church. Present it as a mystery of divine institution, not as a human policy subject to revision.

Practical Connection: Anchor the teaching in real life. Ask participants: What would your spiritual life be like without the ability to confess your sins or receive the Eucharist? Help them appreciate the concrete gift that the priesthood represents in their daily lives rather than treating it as an abstract doctrine.

Hierarchy as Service: Emphasize repeatedly that Holy Orders is not about power or prestige, but about service. The bishop is the "slave of all" in his diocese. The priest wears himself out for his parishioners. Jesus washed feet. Use Mk 10:42-45 to show that Christian authority is radically different from worldly authority. This can be a powerful corrective to both cynicism about Church hierarchy and to misplaced clericalism.

Celibacy (if it comes up): Frame priestly celibacy positively—not as a renunciation of something bad, but as a total gift of self for the Kingdom. The priest forgoes a natural good (marriage and family) for a supernatural one (undivided availability to God and all souls). Note that this is a discipline of the Latin Church, not a dogma, and that Eastern Catholic Churches have a different tradition for parish priests while maintaining celibacy for bishops.

4. Adaptation by Age

Seed (Children): Use the analogy of "Jesus' special helpers." The priest is not God, but God uses his words and hands to bring Jesus to us. Focus on the wonder that Jesus speaks through the priest at Mass and forgives through him in Confession. The children's activity (drawing a priest at Mass) works well here. Keep the tone one of gratitude and awe rather than doctrinal complexity.

Sprouts (Youth): Speak about vocation as a response to a personal call. It is not a career choice but a life-giving surrender. Address cynicism about hierarchy directly—yes, the Church has had bad priests and bishops throughout history, but the sacrament itself remains holy because Christ acts through it. Ask them directly: would you consider that God might be calling you to the priesthood or religious life? Plant the seed even if you don't expect an immediate answer.

Root (New Adults): Go deeper into the responsibility of all the baptized to collaborate with the priestly ministry, not merely "receive" from it. The common priesthood is activated when lay people take ownership of the Church's mission. Explore the complementarity of vocations: marriage, priesthood, religious life, dedicated single life—all different ways of living out the one baptismal call to holiness.

Tree (Adult Formation): Study the writings of St. John Vianney on the priesthood. Explore the theology of apostolic succession and the development of the threefold office (priest, prophet, king). Read key passages from Vatican II's Presbyterorum Ordinis on the nature of the priesthood. Discussion could include: What reforms in priestly formation and lifestyle does the Church need today? How do we balance reverence for the office with honest acknowledgment of the sexual abuse crisis and its causes?

5. Supplementary Resources

  • YouCat: Questions 249-254 on Holy Orders. Excellent for youth and young adult audiences.
  • Catechism: Paragraphs 1533-1600 for thorough preparation. Especially 1546-1553 on the two priesthoods, and 1577-1578 on the male priesthood.
  • Scripture: Jn 20:21-23 (institution), Acts 6:1-6 (ordination of the first deacons), 1 Tim 3:1-13 and Titus 1:5-9 (qualifications for bishops, priests, and deacons), Heb 5:1-10 (Christ the High Priest).
  • Film: "Of Gods and Men" (2010) — a profound meditation on vocation, community, and martyrdom. Suitable for adults and older youth with guidance.
  • Lives of Saints: St. John Vianney, St. John Fisher, St. Charles Borromeo, St. Josemaría Escrivá (on the sanctification of priests and the call to universal holiness).

6. Difficult Questions

Why can't women be priests? The Church's teaching on this is definitive and not open to change. The essential reason is not cultural or sociological but theological and sacramental: the priest acts in persona Christi, representing Christ as the Bridegroom in relation to his Bride, the Church. This spousal symbolism requires a male priest because the sacrament must visibly correspond to the reality it signifies. The Church has no authority to alter the substance of the sacraments as instituted by Christ. This is not a statement about women's worth or abilities—it is about fidelity to the nature of the sacrament itself. Point to St. John Paul II's apostolic letter Ordinatio Sacerdotalis (1994) for the authoritative statement.

What about priests who leave the ministry? They remain priests forever due to the indelible character. Ordination, like Baptism and Confirmation, cannot be undone or repeated. A priest who leaves active ministry (laicization) loses the permission to exercise his priestly functions publicly, but in danger of death he can and should hear confessions and grant absolution. The Church always hopes for his return to active ministry and to the full living of his vocation.

How do we reconcile the holiness of the priesthood with the abuse crisis? The sins of individual priests are grave and have caused terrible harm, but they do not invalidate the sacrament itself. Judas was one of the Twelve, and his betrayal did not nullify the apostolic office. The crisis calls for purification, accountability, and reform—it has led to stronger safeguards in priestly formation and child protection—but the sacrament of Orders remains a gift of Christ to his Church. This is a difficult but essential distinction: we hold priests to the highest standard of holiness precisely because of the sacred office they bear, while also recognizing that the efficacy of the sacraments does not depend on the holiness of the minister.

7. Catechist's Prayer

Lord Jesus, Good Shepherd,
we pray for those whom you have called
to follow you closely in Holy Orders.
May they be holy, courageous, and humble.
May their lives be a living testimony
of your love for us.
And awaken in the hearts of the young
a generous response to the call to the priesthood,
so that your Church may never lack
shepherds after your own heart.
Amen.
