Man and Sin

Big Question

If God created us out of love, why is there so much pain and evil in the world?

«If God exists and is good, why do children suffer?» The question is thrown by Ivan Karamazov in the most profound novel ever written about the problem of evil: Fyodor Dostoevsky's The Brothers Karamazov. Ivan is no ordinary atheist. He is an intellectual who has seen the suffering of the innocent —children tortured, mothers weeping, lives shattered by human cruelty— and says: it is not that he does not believe in God; he is returning his ticket to a world where this is possible.

His brother Alyosha, the young and pure monk, listens in silence. He has no philosophical answer to give him. But Dostoevsky, who had spent four years in a Siberian prison camp for political reasons and had seen evil up close, suggests his answer in the figure of the elder Zosima: evil is not answered with arguments, but with love that takes on the suffering of the other. «There is so much power in active love,» Zosima says. «Do not seek the meaning of suffering; bear it with others.»

The Catechism does not dodge this question. It states clearly that original sin is a reality —a wound in our nature that inclines us toward evil— but that it does not have the last word (CCC 385-390). The last word belongs to Christ, who enters into human suffering and redeems it from within. Dostoevsky understood: the answer is not a theory that explains evil, but a Presence that passes through it.

Activity

Look at your hands. Touch them. Now think: what can your hands do that makes you like God? They can caress, build, write, embrace, create. The image of God is not something abstract: it shows in what you can do out of love. Write down three things you can do today that reflect that you are made in God's image.

But the image of God in us is not merely individual. God is Trinity — a communion of Persons — and we were created for communion. The human being is not made for solitude. «It is not good that the man should be alone» (Gn 2:18). We were created to live in relationship: with God, with others, and with all creation. Our dignity does not depend on what we have or achieve, but on what we are: children of God (CCC 357-360).

Body and Soul

The human being is a unity of body and soul. We are not a spirit trapped in a body, nor a body without a spirit. We are both at once, inseparably. The Catechism states it clearly: «The human person, created in the image of God, is a being at once corporeal and spiritual» (CCC 362). The body is not a prison or an obstacle; it is an essential part of who we are. That is why the resurrection of the body is not a fantasy: God saves us whole, body and soul.

Connection

Read Psalm 8:5-6: «What are human beings that you are mindful of them? You have made them a little lower than angels, and crowned them with glory and honor.» The psalmist marvels that God cares about us. Have you stopped to think that God knows you by name, not by number? What does it mean to you that your body also shares in the dignity of being God's image?

The spiritual soul is what makes us capable of freedom, knowledge, and love. It is not something added to the body; it is the principle that animates it and gives it unity. When the Bible says that God «breathed into his nostrils the breath of life» (Gn 2:7), it is telling us that human life comes directly from God, unlike other creatures. The human being is dust and spirit at the same time: humility and greatness together (CCC 362-365).

Male and Female: Dignity and Equality

God created man and woman with equal dignity. «In the image of God he created them; male and female he created them» (Gn 1:27). Sexual difference is not an accident or a hierarchy; it is part of God's design. Man and woman are equal in dignity and different in complementarity. Together they reflect the image of God in a way that neither can reflect alone (CCC 369-373).

Context

In a world that often confuses equality with uniformity, the Christian faith proposes something different: equal dignity and different gifts. We are not interchangeable; we are complementary. Sin has distorted this complementarity — the domination of man over woman is a consequence of the Fall, not of God's original design (Gn 3:16). Christ restores the original dignity of both.

The Fall: The First Sin

If the human being was created so good, how did evil enter the world? Genesis tells us with a powerful narrative. God placed the man in the garden of Eden and gave him a command: «You may freely eat of every tree of the garden, but of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil you shall not eat, for in the day that you eat of it you shall die» (Gn 2:16-17). The serpent — symbol of the devil — tempts the woman: «Did God say, ‘You shall not eat from any tree in the garden’?» It is not an innocent question; it is a distortion. The serpent sows doubt about God's intentions: «You will not die; for God knows that when you eat of it your eyes will be opened» (Gn 3:4-5) (CCC 385-390).

Original sin is not mere disobedience. It is the decision to want to be like God without God, to determine for oneself what is good and what is evil. It is the root of all sin: pride, distrust in God's love, the desire for absolute autonomy. Adam and Eve did not fall from weakness; they fell from pride. And the consequences were not long in coming: shame, guilt, fear, alienation. «I hid myself because I was naked» (Gn 3:10). Sin breaks the relationship with God, with others, with creation, and with oneself (CCC 396-400).

Going Deeper

Saint Augustine sums up original sin with a famous phrase: «I was made and I unmade myself» («Factus sum et dissipatus sum»). We were not created broken; we broke ourselves. Evil is not a substance that God created; it is a privation, a distortion of the good. The devil, who sinned first, tempted man; but man sinned freely. The Fall is not a distant myth: it repeats every time we choose our own will over God's love (CCC 391-395).

The Consequences of Original Sin

Adam and Eve's sin had devastating consequences. Harmony was broken in every direction. The relationship with God was fractured: instead of walking with him in the cool of the garden, man hides. The relationship between man and woman was distorted: domination replaced communion. The relationship with creation was altered: work became toil, the ground produces thorns. And most profoundly: human nature was wounded. Not destroyed — the image of God was not erased — but wounded. Our intellect was clouded, our will inclined toward evil, concupiscence arose: that inward tendency to sin that we all experience (CCC 400-405).

Saint Paul describes it with an honesty that is painfully familiar: «I do not understand my own actions. For I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate» (Rom 7:15). Does that sound familiar? We all know that experience: we know what is right, but we do the opposite. That inner tension is the wound of original sin. It is not that we are bad by nature; we are good, created in God's image, but we are wounded. And the wound is transmitted to all descendants of Adam, not as personal guilt, but as a condition (CCC 404).

Activity

Imagine a broken mirror. The pieces still reflect the light, but the image is distorted. That is original sin: the image of God in us is still there, but it is wounded. Now imagine someone repairing the mirror with love and patience. Who is that someone? What is his name?

The Promise of Redemption

But God did not stand by with folded arms. Immediately after the Fall, in the midst of punishment, God spoke a promise that changes everything: «I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and hers; he will strike your head» (Gn 3:15). Theologians call this verse the «Protoevangelium» — the first Gospel, the first good news. God does not abandon fallen man; from the very beginning he promises salvation. The woman whose offspring will crush the serpent's head is Mary, and her offspring is Christ (CCC 410).

The history of salvation does not begin in the New Testament. It begins in Genesis. God did not leave us alone in our misery. Sin entered through one man, Adam; grace enters through another man, Christ. Saint Paul says it forcefully: «If the many died through the one man's trespass, much more surely have the grace of God and the free gift in the grace of the one man, Jesus Christ, abounded for the many» (Rom 5:15). Where sin increased, grace abounded all the more (Rom 5:20) (CCC 411-421).

Connection

Compare Genesis 3 with the Annunciation (Lk 1:26-38). In the garden, a woman says «no» to God and sin enters. In Nazareth, a woman says «yes» to God and salvation enters. Mary's obedience undoes Eve's disobedience. The Church Fathers called Mary «the new Eve.» What does this connection between the two women say to you?

Biblical Connection: Genesis 3 — The Fall and the Promise

Read Genesis 3:1-15. The serpent does not force Eve; it tempts her. Temptation always works this way: it distorts God's word, sows doubt about his intentions, offers a shortcut. «Did God really say...?» It is the same strategy used today: «Does God really love you? Are his commandments really for your good?» But notice what happens after the Fall: God does not destroy man. He punishes him, yes — there are real consequences of sin — but he also clothes him («The Lord God made garments of skins for Adam and his wife», Gn 3:21) and makes him a promise (Gn 3:15). Even in punishing, God cares. Even in justice, God loves.

I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and hers; he will strike your head, and you will strike his heel. Genesis 3:15

For Reflection

  1. What strikes you most about the story of the Fall: the temptation, the disobedience, or the consequences? Why?
  2. Saint Paul says: «I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate» (Rom 7:15). In what do you experience that tension in your life?
  3. The serpent distorted God's word to tempt. What «serpents» whisper to you today that God does not love you or that his commandments are oppressive?
  4. God promised salvation immediately after the Fall. What does that tell you about how God responds to your sin?
  5. In what areas of your life do you need Christ to «crush the serpent's head»? What wound do you want him to heal?

Key Takeaway: We were created in God's image, wounded by sin, but never abandoned: where sin increased, grace abounded all the more.

Cultural Resources

Film «The Prince of Egypt» (1998) — The burning bush scene shows Moses falling to his knees before God's presence, who reveals his name. It is a powerful representation of what it means to stand before the God who «is.» But it also reminds us that this same God calls sinners — Moses killed a man — and transforms them. The Fall is not the end of the story.

«The Confessions» by Saint Augustine — The most personal book in the history of theology. Augustine recounts his own Fall: the theft of the pears (he did not need them; he stole for the pleasure of doing evil), his addictions, his search for love in the wrong places. And then, the grace that found him. It is the living account of Romans 7 and 8 together: the struggle with sin and the victory of grace.

Song «Tú Reinas» by Hakuna — A hymn of victory proclaiming that Christ has conquered sin and death. We do not sing the defeat of sin with sadness, but with hope: «Where sin increased, grace abounded all the more.» It is the Christian response to the Fall: not to deny evil, but to celebrate that Christ has already conquered it.
