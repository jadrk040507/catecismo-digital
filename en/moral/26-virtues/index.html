The Virtues

Big Question

What makes you a good person? What you know, or what you do over and over again?

A young pianist arrived at his first lesson with a famous teacher. The teacher asked, «How much do you practice each day?» The young man replied, «Two hours, maestro.» The teacher shook his head: «When I was your age, I practiced eight hours. But now I play without thinking. The scales, the chords, the fingerings... they are part of me. I don’t need to think about each note: my fingers know what to do.» That is what virtues are like. They are like muscles of the soul developed through the repetition of good acts. At first it is hard. You have to think about every movement. But over time, doing good becomes natural. Not because it is easy, but because you have become someone who does good with spontaneity and joy.

What Are Virtues?

The Catechism defines a virtue as «a habitual and firm disposition to do the good» (CCC 1803). It is not an isolated act, but a good habit rooted in the person. Just as a musician is not born knowing how to play but practices until the music flows, so the Christian practices goodness until goodness flows from the heart.

Virtues are acquired through the repetition of good acts. Each time you choose honesty, you become more honest. Each time you master your anger, you become more patient. Virtue is like a path in the mountains: at first it is barely visible, but each time you walk it, it becomes clearer and firmer (CCC 1804-1805).

Activity

Think of a skill you learned through practice: riding a bike, playing an instrument, cooking. At first it was difficult; now you do it almost without thinking. That’s how virtues work. What virtue would you like to «learn» like you learn a song?

The Cardinal Virtues

The Catechism calls four virtues «cardinal» because they are the «hinges» (cardo, in Latin) on which all other human virtues turn (CCC 1805). They are the foundation of the moral life.

Prudence

Prudence is «right reason in action» (CCC 1806). It is the virtue that helps us discern the true good in every situation and choose the right means to achieve it. It is not timidity or indecision: it is practical wisdom. The prudent person knows when to speak and when to be silent, when to act and when to wait. It is the «charioteer of the virtues» because without it, the other virtues can become vices: fortitude becomes recklessness, justice becomes rigidity.

Justice

Justice is «the constant and firm will to give God and neighbor what is due to them» (CCC 1807). It includes justice toward God (worship), toward neighbor (respect for rights), and toward oneself (care for one’s own dignity). It is not just about not stealing: it is giving everyone what they truly deserve, starting with respect and dignity.

Fortitude

Fortitude is the virtue that gives us firmness and constancy in seeking the good, even in the face of difficulties (CCC 1808). It is not the absence of fear, but the ability to act despite fear. The Christian martyr does not stop feeling fear; they feel fear, but they love Christ more. Fortitude allows us to resist temptation and overcome obstacles on the path of good.

Temperance

Temperance is the virtue that moderates the attraction of pleasures and ensures balance in the use of created goods (CCC 1809). It is not repression: it is order. The temperate person is not the one who never enjoys, but the one who knows how to enjoy well. They know when to say «yes» and when to say «no.»

Connection

Read the «Letter to Diognetus» (2nd century): «Christians are not distinguished from other men by country, or language, or clothing... but they live a heavenly life on earth.» The cardinal virtues are precisely that: living on earth in a heavenly way, with prudence, justice, fortitude, and temperance.

The Theological Virtues

If the cardinal virtues are like muscles we develop through exercise, the theological virtues are like the air we breathe: we do not manufacture them; we receive them. They are infused by God into our souls as a gift (CCC 1812-1813).

Faith

Faith is the virtue by which we believe in God and in all that He has revealed (CCC 1814). It is not just believing that God exists (even the demons believe that, Jas 2:19), but trusting in Him, accepting His Word, and living accordingly. Faith is the door to the Christian life.

Hope

Hope is the virtue by which we trust that God will give us eternal life and the means to attain it (CCC 1817). It is not naive optimism: it is certainty that God keeps His promises. Hope sustains us when all seems lost. Like Abraham, who «hoped against hope» (Rom 4:18).

Charity

Charity is the virtue by which we love God above all things and our neighbor as ourselves for love of God (CCC 1822-1829). It is «the bond of perfection» (Col 3:14) and «the end of all commandments» (Rom 13:8-10). Without charity, the other virtues do not lead us to God. Charity is the «soul» of all the virtues.

Deepening

The theological virtues are directly related to God: faith unites us to Him, hope impels us toward Him, charity makes us rest in Him. They are like three threads that weave our relationship with the Most Holy Trinity: faith opens us to the Father, hope sustains us in the Son, charity unites us in the Holy Spirit.

The Gifts and Fruits of the Holy Spirit

Alongside the virtues, the Holy Spirit gives us seven gifts that make us docile to His action: wisdom, understanding, counsel, fortitude, knowledge, piety, and fear of the Lord (CCC 1830-1831). These gifts perfect the virtues and allow us to act under the direct inspiration of the Spirit.

A life guided by the Spirit produces fruits: «love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control» (Gal 5:22-23). They are like the flowers of a well-tended garden: they are not produced by direct effort, but when the conditions are right (CCC 1832).

Biblical Connection: Put On Christ

«Put on then, as God’s chosen ones, holy and beloved, compassion, kindness, humility, meekness, and patience, bearing with one another and, if one has a complaint against another, forgiving each other... And above all these put on love, which binds everything together in perfect harmony» (Col 3:12-14).

St. Peter also invites us to add virtue to virtue: «Supplement your faith with virtue, and virtue with knowledge, and knowledge with self-control, and self-control with steadfastness, and steadfastness with godliness, and godliness with brotherly affection, and brotherly affection with love» (2 Pet 1:5-7). The Christian life is an ascending path where one virtue leads to another. It is not perfectionism: it is letting oneself be transformed by grace.

For Reflection

  1. Which of the cardinal virtues do you feel you need most? Prudence, justice, fortitude, or temperance?
  2. How is your life of faith, hope, and charity? Which of the three theological virtues do you need to cultivate most?
  3. What «spiritual muscle» could you start exercising this week with a concrete act?
  4. Have you ever experienced that doing good becomes easier with practice?
  5. Which gift of the Holy Spirit would you most like to ask for in your prayer?

Key Idea

Key Idea: Virtues are the muscles of the soul. The cardinal ones are trained by the practice of good; the theological ones are received as a gift from God. Together they make us like Christ.

Cultural Treasure

St. John Climacus’s «The Ladder of Divine Ascent» — This 7th-century work describes thirty steps of virtues that the monk must climb to reach union with God. Each step is a virtue that builds on the one before it: detachment, obedience, repentance, meekness, etc. It is a powerful metaphor: virtues are not skipped; they are built step by step.

The Virtues of Notre-Dame Cathedral — On the cathedral’s facade, the virtues are sculpted as women holding emblems: Prudence with a mirror and serpent, Justice with scales and sword, Fortitude with armor and lion, Temperance mixing wine with water. The Christian faith is not abstract: it is embodied in gestures, symbols, and art that educates the eye.
