Catechist's Guide — Our Vocation to Beatitude

Catechist's Guide

For class preparation and delivery. For the catechist's use only.

Class Summary

Estimated duration: 60 minutes

Central theme: God has placed in the human heart the desire for happiness. The beatitudes are the path that Jesus shows us to attain true beatitude.

Objectives:

  • Understand that the desire for happiness comes from God and is satisfied only in him.
  • Know the eight beatitudes of Mt 5:3-12.
  • See the beatitudes as a path to holiness accessible to all.
  • Discover eternal beatitude as the Christian's destiny.

Key verse: «Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven» (Mt 5:3)

CCC references: 1716-1729

Suggested Class Structure

Section Duration What the catechist does
1. Big Question 8 min Story of the young man and the elder. Ask: «What would truly make you happy?»
2. The desire for happiness 10 min St. Augustine's «You have made us for yourself.» Where we seek happiness.
3. The beatitudes 15 min Read Mt 5:3-12. Explain each beatitude, its paradox and promise.
4. Path to holiness 10 min The beatitudes as a universal path to holiness.
5. For Reflection 7 min Share in small groups which beatitude they find most difficult.
6. Closing: Key Takeaway + Prayer 5 min Key Takeaway. Pray the beatitudes prayer together.
7. Week assignment 5 min Hand out the workbook. Invite them to meditate on one beatitude each day.

Notes for the Catechist

The desire for happiness

This is a universal theme that connects with everyone. We all seek happiness, but often in wrong places. Help participants identify their «false promises of happiness» without judging them, simply acknowledging them. Salt water is a good metaphor: it gives thirst instead of quenching it.

The beatitudes as paradox

The beatitudes are countercultural. Jesus did not come to confirm our way of thinking, but to transform it. Help the group feel the force of this paradox: the world says one thing, Jesus says another. It is not about being poor or suffering for its own sake, but about trusting that God has the last word.

Not ethics, but the Kingdom

Present the beatitudes not as another ethical code, but as the manifestation of the Kingdom of God. The Kingdom is already present, though not in its fullness. Living the beatitudes is anticipating heaven.

Eternal beatitude

Heaven is not a «prize» for good people, but the destiny for which we were created. Help participants to have hope and to see the present life as a path toward that goal.

Sensitivity with suffering

Some participants may be confused by «blessed are those who mourn» or «blessed are the persecuted.» Clarify that suffering is not good in itself, but that God can transform it and give it meaning. It is not a call to pain, but to trust.

Age Adaptation

🌱 Seed (ages 7-12)

Use the image of a hidden treasure: the beatitudes are the treasure Jesus shows us. Activity: draw their favorite beatitude. Memorize with gestures «Blessed are the pure in heart.»

🌿 Sprouts (ages 13-17)

Connect with their search for happiness in social media, success, popularity. The beatitudes are a radical alternative path. Ask: «Have you ever felt that success does not give the happiness it promised?»

🪴 Root (adults new to the faith)

Many adults have experienced the frustration of placing their happiness in things that do not last. The beatitudes offer real hope. Help them examine where they have placed their trust and redirect it toward God.

🌳 Tree (formed adults)

Go deeper into the theology of beatitude: the beatific vision in St. Thomas Aquinas, the lumen gloriae, the relationship between grace and glory. Discuss how the beatitudes are the ethics of the Kingdom that anticipates eternal life.

Difficult Questions

«Does this mean Christians should not be happy in this life?»

No. The beatitudes do not deny earthly happiness, but reorient it. God wants our happiness, but an authentic happiness that does not depend on external circumstances. The saints are the happiest people who have ever existed: their joy came not from having everything, but from having God.

«Are the poor automatically blessed?»

No. Material poverty is not automatically a beatitude. Jesus says «poor in spirit»: those who recognize their need for God, who do not trust in their own strength but in God. A rich person can be «poor in spirit» if they live with humility and detachment. A poor person may not be if they are bitter or envious.

«What about those who suffer unjustly? Are they blessed even if they do not believe?»

The beatitudes are promises of the Kingdom for those who follow Christ. But God does not abandon anyone who suffers. His mercy extends to all. The beatitude of those who mourn is a promise of comfort for all who suffer, though perhaps only believers can fully understand its meaning.

Catechist's Prayer

Lord Jesus, Master of the beatitudes,
teach me to live as you live.
Give me a poor and humble heart,
meek and merciful,
pure and peaceful.
May my life be an announcement of your Kingdom,
and may I help others discover
that true happiness is found in you.
Amen.
