Jesus Christ, God and Man

Big Question

Who do you say that I am?

Jesus asked his disciples in Caesarea Philippi. Before, he had asked what people said about him; they answered respectable things: "A prophet," "John the Baptist raised," "Elijah." None were completely wrong, but none reached the center. Then Jesus asked them the question he also asks us: "But who do you say that I am?" (Mk 8:29). It is not enough what others say. It is not enough to give an inherited answer. The Christian faith hinges on this question: Who is Jesus? Because if Jesus is only a moral teacher, we can admire him; but if he is God made man, everything changes. We can no longer just admire him: we must follow him, worship him, entrust ourselves to him. The Catechism states it clearly: "The Christian faith is not a "religion of the book". Christianity is the religion of the "Word" of God, not a written and mute word, but the Word made flesh and living" (CCC 108).

The name that says it all: Jesus, God saves

"Jesus" means in Hebrew "God saves." It is not just any name or an honorary title. It is a declaration of mission: this man exists to save. Before his birth, the angel tells Joseph: "You are to give him the name Jesus, because he will save his people from their sins" (Mt 1:21). The name already contains the entire program of his life. He did not come to teach philosophy, or to found an NGO, or to propose a lifestyle. He came to save us. And not from a political or social problem, but from sin, which is the root of all evil (CCC 430-435).

Another title given to him is "Christ," which means "Anointed" in Greek, the same as "Messiah" in Hebrew. Jesus is the one anointed by the Holy Spirit for the mission of salvation. He is not a political messiah as many expected in his time, but the Messiah who fulfills all of God's promises: king, prophet, and priest at once (CCC 436-440).

Activity: Write your full name and look up its meaning. Now think: if the name Jesus means "God saves," what does that say about his identity? And about his mission?

True God: the eternal Son of the Father

Jesus is not merely a man close to God. He is God himself made man. The prologue of John states it with clarity that leaves no room for doubt: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God... And the Word became flesh and made his dwelling among us" (Jn 1:1,14). The Word —the Logos, the Son— existed from all eternity. He was not created. He is God, consubstantial with the Father. And he became flesh: he entered our history, our time, our body (CCC 422-423).

The Council of Nicaea (325) formulated this truth with precision: Jesus is "begotten, not made, of the same substance as the Father." He is not an exceptional creature, nor a being intermediary between God and human beings. He is God of God, Light of Light, true God of true God. Everything that the Father is, the Son is, except being Father (CCC 464-465).

Jesus himself says it without ambiguity: "I and the Father are one" (Jn 10:30). And to Philip who asks to see the Father: "Anyone who has seen me has seen the Father" (Jn 14:9). It is not a metaphor. It is a real identity: in Jesus, God himself comes to meet us (CCC 466).

Going deeper: The heresy of Arius (4th century) denied the divinity of Christ: it said the Son was the first creature of God, superior to the angels but not God. The Council of Nicaea condemned this heresy because, if Jesus is not true God, then on the cross it was not God who saved us, but a creature. Salvation is the work of God himself, not an intermediary. If someone saves you from drowning, you do not want them to send a representative; you want them to come personally. That is what God did in Christ.

True man: like us in all things but sin

But Jesus is not only God. He is also truly man. Not the appearance of a man, not a divine disguise, but a real man: born of a woman, grew up in a town, learned a trade, was hungry and thirsty, wept at the tomb of his friend Lazarus, felt anguish in Gethsemane, experienced abandonment on the cross. The Catechism states it firmly: "The Son of God... assumed a real and complete human nature... truly man, like us in all things but sin" (CCC 470-471).

The Council of Chalcedon (451) defined that Jesus has two natures —divine and human— united in one Person (the hypostatic union). Neither confused nor separated. He is not "half God and half man," nor "God with a human body." He is 100% God and 100% man, without division or mixture (CCC 467-468).

This matters profoundly. If Jesus were not God, his death could not save us. If he were not man, he could not represent us or have compassion on us. But because he is true God and true man, he can unite God and human beings in his own Person. He is the bridge, the mediator, the only one who can save (CCC 469, 480).

Activity: Read Heb 4:15: "We do not have a high priest who is unable to empathize with our weaknesses, but we have one who has been tempted in every way, just as we are—yet he did not sin." What does this sentence tell you? How does your prayer change knowing that Jesus understands what you feel?

The kenosis: God emptied himself to love us

Saint Paul describes the mystery of the incarnation with a surprising word: kenosis, emptying. "Who, being in very nature God, did not consider equality with God something to be used to his own advantage; rather, he made himself nothing by taking the very nature of a servant, being made in human likeness" (Phil 2:6-7). God did not come in power and glory. He came in fragility: as a baby in a manger, as a carpenter in a forgotten town, as a condemned man on a cross. He emptied himself not because he ceased to be God, but because he chose not to cling to the privileges of his divinity (CCC 471-472).

The kenosis is not a weakness of God, but the most radical expression of his love. True love empties itself, gives itself, descends. God did not love us from a distance: he entered our flesh, our pain, our death. And therefore "God exalted him to the highest place and gave him the name that is above every name, that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow" (Phil 2:9-10). The exaltation comes after the emptying. The glory passes through the cross (CCC 473-474).

Reflection: If God emptied himself to love us, what does he ask of us? How do we live kenosis in our daily lives: in the family, at work, in the community?

Biblical Connection: Peter's Confession

In Mark 8:27-30, Jesus walks with his disciples toward the villages of Caesarea Philippi and asks them: "Who do people say I am?" The answers vary: John the Baptist, Elijah, one of the prophets. Then Jesus goes to the heart: "But what about you? Who do you say I am?" Peter responds with the most important confession in the Gospels: "You are the Messiah." It is the moment when a human being recognizes in Jesus the One sent by God. But Peter still does not understand what kind of Messiah Jesus is; when Jesus announces his passion, Peter rejects it and Jesus rebukes him: "Get behind me, Satan!" (Mk 8:33). Knowing that Jesus is the Messiah is the first step; discovering that the Messiah suffers and dies for us is the second. The Christian faith is not complete with Peter's confession, but with the cross and resurrection (CCC 442).

For Reflection

  1. Who is Jesus to you? A great teacher, a prophet, or God made man? What changes in your life if you really believe he is God?
  2. Jesus experienced hunger, fatigue, weeping, anguish, and temptation. How does it affect you to know that God understands your pain from within, not just from above?
  3. The kenosis tells us that God emptied himself for love. In what areas of your life do you need to "empty" your ego, your pride, your need for control?
  4. Peter confessed that Jesus was the Messiah, but did not understand the cross. Is there something in your faith that you still do not understand or find hard to accept?
  5. "I and the Father are one" (Jn 10:30). If Jesus is truly God and truly man, what does it mean to follow him? Is it optional or the most important decision of your life?

Key Takeaway: Jesus is not half God and half man. He is true God and true man. And that is precisely the mystery that saves us.

Cultural Enrichment

"Christ of San Clemente" by Velázquez — This crucifix painted by Velázquez (c. 1632) shows Christ on the cross with a shattering humanity. It is not a distant God suffering symbolically; it is a real man, with the body marked by nails, the head fallen, real blood. Velázquez painted the divinity hidden beneath the bleeding flesh. It is the kenosis made image.

Film "The Passion of the Christ" (2004) — By Mel Gibson. Perhaps the most raw cinematic representation of the humanity of Christ. Watching it is not easy, but it helps to understand something that the Catechism emphasizes: Jesus suffered truly. It was not a representation. It was not a symbol. God entered our pain to the ultimate consequences.

Song "I Need You, Jesus" by Hakuna — Expresses the radical trust of the Christian in Christ. It is not a generic trust in "something superior," but in a concrete Person who is God and man, who knows us and loves us. It is a good way to enter into the prayer of this lesson.
